## 1. Offering Definition

### Working name

**Systems Modernization Assessment**

### Proposed positioning

A bounded, project-based engagement that helps an organization understand how
its current applications, infrastructure, data, integrations, and workflows
support—or obstruct—business operations. The assessment identifies material
constraints, dependencies, inefficiencies, and relevant technology risks, then
translates the findings into prioritized recommendations and a practical
modernization roadmap.

The engagement begins with business operations rather than a predetermined
technology purchase. Modernization may involve improving existing tools,
connecting systems, simplifying workflows, correcting ownership or
configuration problems, planning an incremental replacement, or defining a
focused new capability. It does not presume a wholesale replacement, cloud
migration, automation, AI implementation, or custom software project.

### Core customer value

The proposed assessment gives decision-makers a clearer, evidence-based basis
for deciding:

- what is making an important operation difficult, slow, fragile, or opaque;
- which systems, data, integrations, people, and vendors the operation depends
  on;
- which constraints and risks must be addressed before implementation;
- which improvements are likely to be practical and proportionate;
- what should happen first, later, or not at all; and
- whether Aston Cyber, another specialist, an internal team, or a vendor should
  perform the follow-on work.

This is a proposed methodology and offering model. It is not evidence of a
completed Aston Cyber client engagement or a promise of a particular outcome.

## 2. Intended Customer Profile

### Primary audience

- established small and midsize organizations whose operations depend on
  multiple business applications, infrastructure services, data stores, or
  manual handoffs;
- larger organizations with a specific bounded modernization need that can be
  evaluated independently of a large enterprise program;
- owners, executives, operational leaders, and technology leaders who need a
  practical decision path rather than a general transformation strategy;
- organizations willing to make appropriate stakeholders, documentation, and
  system evidence available; and
- organizations that value vendor-independent recommendations, explicit
  limitations, security considerations, and maintainable implementation.

### Relevant business conditions

The assessment may be useful when:

- important information is divided among systems, spreadsheets, inboxes, or
  undocumented workarounds;
- employees repeat data entry, reconcile inconsistent records, or assemble
  reports manually;
- a critical application is aging, fragile, poorly understood, or difficult to
  support;
- integration failures or unclear ownership create operational delays;
- a migration or replacement is being considered without a reliable dependency
  map;
- current tools may be capable but are poorly configured or disconnected;
- leadership is considering automation or applied AI without a sufficiently
  defined process, data foundation, or risk model;
- a modernization initiative needs security requirements and operational risks
  identified before design or procurement; or
- an organization needs an independent roadmap before engaging an implementer
  or product vendor.

## 3. Qualification and Disqualification

### Qualification indicators

An opportunity is a plausible fit when:

- there is a defined business operation, capability, or decision to assess;
- an accountable sponsor can clarify priorities and make scope decisions;
- relevant process owners and technical stakeholders can participate;
- the organization can provide lawful and authorized access to appropriate
  documentation, configurations, demonstrations, and personnel;
- the environment can be bounded sufficiently for responsible analysis;
- decision-makers accept that recommendations may favor improving existing
  systems over buying or building new technology; and
- the expected result is an assessment and decision artifact, not an implicit
  guarantee of implementation or business performance.

### Conditions requiring qualification or an optional extension

- substantial regulated, controlled, sensitive, or classified information;
- specialized platforms or infrastructure outside demonstrated expertise;
- environments requiring formal audit, attestation, certification, legal
  interpretation, or authorized offensive security testing;
- extensive physical-site discovery, travel, or on-premises inspection;
- large multi-entity or multinational environments;
- a need for detailed source-code, data-quality, licensing, cost, or vendor
  contract analysis; or
- a scope that requires multiple specialist disciplines or sustained program
  staffing.

These conditions do not automatically disqualify an engagement. They require a
specific scope decision, appropriate expertise, and explicit authorization.

### Disqualification indicators

The proposed assessment is not a fit when the customer primarily requires:

- comprehensive managed IT ownership or help-desk support;
- 24/7 monitoring, MDR, SOC, or emergency incident response;
- a regulatory audit, attestation, certification, legal opinion, or guaranteed
  compliance result;
- an unbounded enterprise transformation or large-program staffing model;
- a predetermined product recommendation without independent evaluation;
- unsupported access to systems, data, accounts, or third-party environments;
- a guaranteed savings, delivery, security, availability, or business outcome;
  or
- immediate implementation before material dependencies and risks can be
  understood.

## 4. Assessment Scope Model

### 4.1 Practical core scope

Every assessment should include a deliberately bounded version of the following
core work. Depth varies with the agreed business problem and available evidence.

#### A. Business objective and operating context

- define the operation, capability, or decision being assessed;
- identify the accountable sponsor, process owners, technology owners, and
  materially affected users;
- document desired improvements, current constraints, and decision deadlines;
  and
- establish the assessment boundary, assumptions, and known exclusions.

#### B. Current-state technology inventory

- identify in-scope applications, infrastructure, platforms, data stores, and
  material third-party services;
- record the stated purpose, owner, users, support model, and lifecycle concern
  for each material component;
- identify available architecture, process, vendor, and operational
  documentation; and
- distinguish confirmed evidence, stakeholder statements, assumptions, and
  unknowns.

#### C. Dependencies and information movement

- map material system-to-system, user-to-system, and vendor dependencies;
- identify significant data sources, destinations, transfers, and manual
  handoffs;
- note brittle integrations, duplicated records, unclear systems of record, and
  single points of operational dependency; and
- identify practical integration or simplification opportunities without
  presuming a particular platform.

#### D. Workflows and operational bottlenecks

- document one or more in-scope workflows at a level sufficient for decisions;
- identify repeated entry, waiting, rework, inconsistent approval, manual
  reconciliation, and reporting friction;
- distinguish process problems from configuration, integration, data, training,
  ownership, and tooling problems; and
- identify where human judgment, review, or exception handling must remain.

#### E. Technology and cybersecurity risk

- identify material ownership, access, data-protection, maintainability,
  resilience, vendor, configuration, and lifecycle concerns visible within the
  agreed scope;
- consider how proposed modernization options could create or reduce risk;
- identify evidence gaps that prevent a reliable conclusion; and
- distinguish a focused technology-risk review from an audit, attestation,
  penetration test, legal opinion, or comprehensive security assessment.

#### F. Recommendations and roadmap

- define practical improvement options, including process or configuration
  changes that do not require new technology;
- explain significant alternatives, dependencies, assumptions, and tradeoffs;
- prioritize recommendations using agreed business value, risk, feasibility,
  dependency, and readiness considerations;
- identify prerequisites and early validation activities; and
- sequence immediate decisions, near-term improvements, later initiatives, and
  explicitly deferred work.

### 4.2 Optional extensions

Optional extensions should be included only when needed and explicitly scoped:

- deeper application or infrastructure architecture review;
- cloud or migration readiness;
- identity and access review;
- Microsoft 365 or cloud configuration review;
- data-quality, data-governance, or reporting analysis;
- integration/API feasibility analysis;
- automation opportunity assessment;
- applied AI readiness and human-oversight design;
- vendor/product option analysis;
- focused custom-software feasibility;
- cybersecurity architecture or control review;
- cost-model inputs or total-cost considerations based on reliable customer
  data;
- implementation risk, transition, testing, or rollback planning;
- onsite discovery, if delivery capacity and terms are separately approved;
- prototype or proof-of-concept definition; or
- specialist participation where the environment requires expertise outside
  the core scope.

Optional extensions are not implied by the core offering and may change the
commercial scope, evidence requirements, and delivery model.

## 5. Required Customer Inputs and Access

The exact request should be minimized to what the agreed scope requires. Likely
inputs include:

- an accountable sponsor and a primary coordination contact;
- access to process owners, system owners, administrators, and representative
  users;
- current business objectives, constraints, priorities, and known concerns;
- available application, infrastructure, integration, data-flow, process,
  vendor, licensing, and support documentation;
- demonstrations of in-scope systems and workflows;
- approved read-only configuration evidence or exports where necessary;
- relevant inventories, reports, issue histories, or change records;
- known contractual, regulatory, security, privacy, retention, or operational
  constraints; and
- timely review of factual findings and identified assumptions.

### Access principles

- Access must be authorized, least-privilege, time-bounded where practical, and
  appropriate to the assessment.
- Customer credentials should not be transmitted or retained unnecessarily.
- Production changes are outside the assessment unless separately authorized as
  a follow-on implementation.
- Sensitive information should be minimized, handled according to agreed terms,
  and excluded when it is unnecessary to reach the assessment objective.
- Inability to obtain material evidence must be recorded as a limitation rather
  than filled with assumptions.
- Third-party access requires confirmation that the customer has authority to
  provide it.

## 6. Proposed Assessment Methodology

The assessment uses the approved Understand and Evaluate stages. Implement and
Improve are possible follow-on models, not implied assessment work.

### Stage 1: Understand

1. Confirm the business objective, sponsor, decision, and scope boundary.
2. Identify stakeholders and evidence sources.
3. Gather available documentation and current-state information.
4. Conduct focused stakeholder and workflow discovery.
5. Record facts, assumptions, unknowns, and constraints separately.

### Stage 2: Evaluate

1. Build the bounded technology, dependency, and information-flow view.
2. Analyze operational bottlenecks and their likely causes.
3. Identify relevant technology and cybersecurity risks.
4. Develop plausible improvement options without presuming replacement.
5. Evaluate dependencies, feasibility, risks, ownership, and tradeoffs.
6. Prioritize recommendations and sequence a practical roadmap.
7. Review factual findings and material assumptions with the customer.
8. Present the decision brief and agreed deliverables.

### Method principles

- business operations before technology selection;
- current-state evidence before target-state design;
- improvement of existing capabilities before unnecessary replacement;
- security, maintainability, ownership, and handoff by design;
- independent judgment without vendor commission or resale incentive;
- explicit differentiation among facts, stakeholder statements, assumptions,
  professional judgment, and unresolved unknowns; and
- recommendations proportionate to the assessed problem and evidence.

## 7. Representative Deliverables Inventory

### Core deliverables

The final commercial scope should select and tailor appropriate artifacts. A
proposed core set is:

1. **Executive decision brief** — business problem, material findings,
   decision context, recommended direction, and immediate actions.
2. **Assessment scope and evidence record** — systems and workflows examined,
   stakeholder inputs, evidence sources, assumptions, exclusions, and
   limitations.
3. **Current-state systems inventory** — material applications,
   infrastructure, data stores, services, owners, purposes, and lifecycle or
   support observations.
4. **Dependency and information-flow view** — material integrations, manual
   transfers, systems of record, external dependencies, and significant gaps.
5. **Workflow findings** — bounded current-state workflows, bottlenecks,
   control points, handoffs, exception paths, and likely causes.
6. **Risk and constraints register** — relevant operational, integration,
   maintainability, data, access, security, vendor, and delivery concerns,
   including evidence gaps.
7. **Prioritized recommendations** — proposed actions with rationale,
   dependencies, tradeoffs, prerequisites, and an indication of relative
   priority rather than unsupported financial promises.
8. **Modernization roadmap** — practical sequence of decisions, validation,
   foundational improvements, implementation candidates, and deferred work.
9. **Leadership readout** — presentation and discussion of findings,
   limitations, options, and next decisions.

### Optional deliverables

- future-state workflow or architecture view;
- integration opportunity matrix;
- automation or applied AI opportunity register;
- migration-readiness or transition plan;
- security architecture or focused control plan;
- vendor evaluation criteria or decision matrix;
- implementation backlog or work-package definitions;
- validation, testing, rollback, or adoption considerations;
- proof-of-concept brief; or
- specialist findings clearly attributed to the responsible discipline.

### Deliverable quality requirements

Each deliverable must:

- identify its scope, evidence basis, date, and material limitations;
- be understandable by its intended executive, operational, or technical
  audience;
- avoid implying audit assurance or certainty beyond the evidence;
- identify ownership and dependencies where they affect actionability;
- use consistent names for systems, workflows, risks, and recommendations; and
- remain useful if the customer selects another qualified implementer.

## 8. Prioritization and Implementation Sequencing

The roadmap should use qualitative criteria agreed during scoping, such as:

- importance to the business operation;
- severity and likelihood of the relevant operational or technology risk;
- dependency on other decisions or foundational work;
- feasibility given people, access, data, platform, and vendor constraints;
- reversibility and ability to validate incrementally;
- maintainability and clarity of ownership; and
- readiness for implementation.

Recommendations should be grouped in a practical sequence:

1. **Clarify or stabilize:** resolve ownership, evidence gaps, immediate
   fragility, or prerequisite decisions.
2. **Connect or simplify:** improve configuration, integrations, information
   flow, and workflows where current tools remain suitable.
3. **Modernize or replace:** plan bounded migration, custom capability, or
   platform change where evidence supports it.
4. **Validate and improve:** verify the result, document ownership, and define
   proportionate ongoing review.

This sequence is a planning model, not a promise that every engagement will
contain four implementation stages.

## 9. Appropriate Follow-On Engagements

The assessment may stand alone. A customer may implement the roadmap internally,
with an existing provider, with a specialist, or through a separately scoped
Aston Cyber engagement.

Potential follow-on work, subject to fit and separate agreement, includes:

- architecture or detailed implementation planning;
- application and data integration;
- cloud, infrastructure, or system migration;
- workflow or reporting automation;
- bounded custom software development;
- applied AI evaluation, prototype, or human-reviewed workflow;
- security architecture, control implementation, or configuration improvement;
- implementation validation, documentation, and handoff; or
- defined ongoing advisory and incremental improvement.

No follow-on service is required. Ongoing work does not imply managed IT,
dedicated staffing, 24/7 coverage, emergency response, or guaranteed immediate
availability.

## 10. Scope Limitations and Exclusions

Unless explicitly added through an approved scope, the Systems Modernization
Assessment excludes:

- implementation, configuration changes, migration, deployment, or production
  remediation;
- comprehensive enterprise architecture or transformation-program ownership;
- exhaustive discovery of every system, workflow, data element, dependency, or
  risk outside the agreed boundary;
- formal regulatory, financial, privacy, accessibility, or security audit;
- certification, attestation, legal advice, or compliance guarantee;
- penetration testing, vulnerability exploitation, red-team activity, or
  incident response;
- continuous monitoring, SOC, MDR, help desk, managed IT, or operational
  ownership;
- procurement, product resale, vendor representation, or vendor-funded
  recommendation;
- source-code review, data migration analysis, cost modeling, contract review,
  or licensing analysis unless specifically included;
- detailed implementation design, statements of work, fixed estimates, or
  delivery commitments for follow-on projects;
- guaranteed savings, revenue, staffing change, risk elimination, security,
  compliance, delivery, or performance results;
- access to systems or information the customer is not authorized to provide;
- use or disclosure of unnecessary confidential, personal, controlled, or
  classified information; and
- responsibility for third-party products, services, availability, licensing,
  or roadmap decisions.

Findings are limited to the agreed scope, available evidence, assessment date,
and conditions observed or represented during the engagement.
